About

Economist working on trade, globalization and labour markets in Scandinavia, with a particular focus on the Øresund region.

Background

I was born and raised in Iceland, studied economics at the University of Iceland, with an exchange year at the University of Oklahoma, and at Boston University, and worked as an economist in Reykjavik before moving to Lund for my doctoral studies in 2011.

In Lund, with Copenhagen a short train ride across the bridge, I became interested early in the Øresund region: two countries, two sets of institutions and a labour market that increasingly spans both. That interest has shaped my research for well over a decade.

Most of my work builds on Danish and Swedish administrative microdata. Over the years I have built a database that merges and links these registers, with multiple links across the border. The result is a unique dataset, and it underpins several of my research projects, including work on scale economies in global value chains, knowledge spillovers from cross-border commuters and multinational firms, as well as a number of others.

Björn Thor Arnarson is an economist at the ROCKWOOL Foundation Research Unit in Copenhagen who studies trade, globalization and labour markets in Scandinavia and the Øresund region.

Björn Thor Arnarson is a researcher at the ROCKWOOL Foundation Research Unit in Copenhagen and an affiliated researcher at the Department of Economics, Lund University. He uses Danish and Swedish administrative data to study how globalization, trade and knowledge spillovers affect firms and workers, with a particular focus on the integrated labour market of the Øresund region. His research has appeared in the Journal of International Economics and the Scandinavian Journal of Economics.

Research in plain language

Growing Together and Apart

As firms grow, they hand more tasks to their suppliers, and the workforces of firms and suppliers become more specialized. Using Danish data that link firms’ cross-border trade to their employees, we show that this outsourcing makes firms markedly more productive: without it, average labour productivity would be 25 percent lower.

Globalization and the Gender Gap in Mental Well-being

When a Danish firm trades with partners in distant time zones, work spills outside normal hours. In such firms, women become more likely than men to use antidepressants, with the largest effects among college-educated workers under 45 and single mothers.

Knowledge Spillovers from Cross-Border Commuters

The Øresund Bridge turned Copenhagen and Malmö into one labour market. Danish firms that took on more Swedish commuters became more productive, and the Danish colleagues working alongside the commuters earned more, consistent with knowledge travelling across the border.
